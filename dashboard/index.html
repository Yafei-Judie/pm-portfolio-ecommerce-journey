02 · Delivery

Lateness isn't random — it's geography

Olist Brazilian e-commerce dataset, ~100k real orders 2016–2018, loaded into local SQLite.

Lateness by state

All 27 states with 30+ delivered orders
Read: São Paulo — 42% of all volume — sits at a relatively good 4.5% late. Alagoas, Maranhão, and Sergipe run 15–21% late. This is a coverage problem concentrated in specific regions.

Lateness by seller-customer distance

Real haversine distance, computed from zip-prefix geolocation
Read: same-metro deliveries run late 4.4% of the time. Past 1,500km, that nearly triples to 11.7%. Physical distance is a real mechanism behind the state-level pattern, not just a coincidence of geography.
Sequencing note

São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro together account for roughly half of all late orders by volume, not rate — SP ships 40,494 orders at 4.5% late (~1,822 late); RJ ships 12,350 at 12.1% late (~1,494 late), nearly as many from a third the volume. That's a scoped, answerable ops question (why does RJ underperform SP by 3x?) worth running in parallel with the notification feature below, not instead of it.

The finding this dashboard almost missed

97.3% of reviews in the "7+ days late" bucket were submitted before the package arrived. Olist's review survey triggers off the estimated delivery date, not actual receipt — so for the bucket the whole feature is built on, the review is mostly measuring "still overdue," not "arrived late and I'm upset." That doesn't kill the case for a notification. It changes what to measure: compare against when the review was submitted relative to the notification, not against final delivery date. 04 · Rigor

Pressure-testing the finding, not just reporting it

A finding that only shows the angle supporting the roadmap decision is less credible than one checked from other directions. Four independent agents, each given the real data, argued a different lens against the core claim — then every load-bearing number they produced was independently re-run against the live database before it went in this repo.

Causal inference

Is this causation, or do bad sellers just produce both slow shipping and bad reviews? The 1–2 star share is more defensible than the mean-score gap — a 1–5 scale isn't linear, and "costs 2.6 points" implies a precision the data doesn't support.

Business strategy

The metric that moved most (review score) isn't the metric that matters most (revenue). Repeat-purchase impact is real but small. An ops fix in RJ may have a higher ceiling than a comms feature — worth running in parallel, not instead.

Customer behavior

A notification can backfire two ways: manufacturing concern over a mild delay, or "double deviation" if the revised estimate itself slips. v1's trigger is a weak heuristic — the message needs to show a range, not a confident-sounding date.

Measurement quality

Found the review-timing issue above. Also: 0.67% of delivered orders have no matching review and get silently dropped by the join; 547 orders have duplicate review rows. Neither changes the headline, both should be stated.
How this critique pass actually worked
Four agents ran in parallel, each reading the real repo files directly and arguing one lens against the same finding — not generic disclaimers, concrete alternative explanations using the actual numbers. A fifth agent synthesized all four into analysis/limitations-and-alternative-views.md. Every specific number that came out of that pass (the 97.3% review-timing figure, the 646 missing reviews, the 547 duplicates) was then independently re-run against the live SQLite database by hand before being trusted — two of the agent's numbers were off by small amounts and got corrected to the verified figures shown here. The PRD itself was then edited to match: softened from "confirmed the premise cleanly" to what a two-variable crosstab can actually support, with two real scope changes (the success metric, and a message-confidence requirement) rather than just an appended disagreement.
