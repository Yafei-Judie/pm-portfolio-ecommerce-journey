# Limitations and alternative views

The PRD in `prd/proactive-delivery-notifications-prd.md` treats `sql/04`'s delay-vs-review numbers as a confirmed premise. Section 8 says the query "confirmed the premise cleanly." A finding that only shows the angle that supports the roadmap decision is less credible than one that's been checked from other directions, so this section pressure-tests the core claim: does late delivery cause bad reviews, is a notification the right response, and is the data even measuring what the PRD assumes it's measuring. Four lenses, each below, some of which change what should ship and some of which don't.

## Causal inference: association, not proven cause

`sql/04` is a two-variable crosstab: delivery timing against review score. No seller_id, no product_category, no freight or distance control. That's enough to show a strong association, not enough to show delay is what's causing the score.

The strongest alternative explanation is a common cause: seller and fulfillment quality. Olist is a marketplace of many independent sellers, each choosing their own carrier and packaging. `sql/03` already shows lateness is concentrated by geography, AL at 21.4%, MA at 17.4%, SE at 15.2%, versus SP at 4.5% on 42% of volume. That's not just distance, it's a proxy for which sellers a customer landed with. Lower-volume, non-SP-hub states are more likely served by smaller, less professionalized sellers with longer, more error-prone logistics chains, more handoffs, more chances for damage or a wrong-item pick, not just more elapsed days. Olist's review score covers the whole order. A customer who got a damaged item and waited 10 extra days has no way to split their 1-star between "the wait" and "the product arrived broken." Freight running 20-30% of item price in electronics, furniture, and food/drink flags exactly the categories where fulfillment is hardest, fragile or perishable goods are both more likely to be delayed and more likely to arrive damaged, independent of timing. There's also a reverse-direction story worth naming: an order delayed because a seller is scrambling (stockout, a bad item caught and re-picked) has its problem causing the delay, not the delay causing the dissatisfaction. The bad review would have happened anyway.

The real test is rerunning the bucket comparison within seller_id and within product_category, same seller, same product type, late versus on-time only. If the 4.3-versus-1.7 gap survives that control, delay-as-cause gets much more credible. As written, the query can't tell "customers punish waiting" apart from "bad sellers produce both slow shipping and bad products."

There's a second issue with how the gap gets described. A 1-5 star rating is ordinal and typically J-shaped in real systems, heavy at 1 and 5, thin in the middle. Saying delay "costs 2.6 points" (the PRD's Goal section frames the 1.70-to-2.71 move this way) implies a linear unit that doesn't exist here. The 1-2 star share is the more defensible number for the same reason findings.md leads with it: it doesn't assume interval scaling, and it's a rate you can attach a real cost to (refund rate, CX ticket volume). 79.2% versus 8.9% is something you can build a cost model on. 2.6 average points is not.

One more number belongs in this conversation: repeat purchase. Customers whose first order was late repeat at 2.55%, versus 3.03% for on-time, about a 0.5-point absolute gap. Real and directionally consistent, but small next to the review-score swing, and the baseline itself is only about 3% repeat purchase across all customers regardless of timing. This is a structurally low-repeat marketplace. If the PRD wants a revenue case, not just a review-score case, the addressable upside on retention is capped at roughly half a percentage point of an already-thin base. That belongs in the PRD and currently isn't there.

## Business strategy: the metric that moved most isn't the metric that matters most

Review score is a proxy. Checked against an actual revenue behavior, repeat purchase, the two disagree sharply on size. Late orders crash from ~4.3 to 1.70 in review score, a roughly 60% collapse. Repeat purchase moves from 3.03% to 2.55% for the same comparison, a 0.48-point absolute gap, about 16% relative. Real, same direction, nowhere near as dramatic. The PRD is optimizing the number that moved the most, not the number that matters most for revenue.

Read the PRD's own goal statement closely: at best, the notification moves some of the 7+-day-late bucket from 1.70 toward the 1-7-day bucket's 2.71. That's the entire addressable upside by construction, a message can't make a late package arrive on time. Two limits follow. It only touches the 6.7% of orders (6,409 of 96,353) that are already late, the other 93.3% see no benefit. And its own ceiling, 2.71, is still far below the 4.1-4.3 that on-time orders get. Notification is damage control. The bigger score jump in the data is late-versus-not-late, not informed-late-versus-silent-late, and a communication feature can never reach that first lever by design.

The state data points at where an operational fix would actually pay off, and it's not where the percentage table makes it look. AL's 21.4% late rate looks alarming, but it's 397 total orders, about 85 late. SP and RJ alone account for roughly half of all late orders nationally, not because their rates are worst but because their volume is so large. SP delivers 40,494 orders at 4.5% late (~1,822 late orders); RJ delivers 12,350 at 12.1% late (~1,494 late orders), nearly as many late orders as SP despite a third of the volume. That reframes the ops question from "fix logistics everywhere" to "why does RJ underperform SP," a scoped, answerable question, not a diffuse mandate. One open question the data doesn't resolve: whether Olist controls carrier selection directly or sellers choose their own logistics, since this is a marketplace, not a single retailer. If it's the latter, "fix carrier selection" is a seller-policy lever, not an engineering fix, and should be checked before committing a roadmap slot to either version.

None of this means skip the notification. It's cheap, rules-based, no ML per the PRD's own scope, and it has a real secondary benefit already listed in the PRD, cutting "where is my order" support volume. If sellers control carrier choice, the notification may be the only lever the platform actually has. The argument is sequencing: a short, two-market ops diagnostic (why does RJ run 12.1% against SP's 4.5%) could tell the team within a sprint whether a higher-ceiling fix exists before the roadmap slot goes to the lower-ceiling one.

## Customer behavior: notification can help or backfire, and the PRD doesn't scope the part that decides which

Most of the pain in a late-order wait isn't the delay, it's the uncertainty. An unexplained, open-ended wait feels worse than an explained one with a known new date, even at the same actual duration. That's the case for the notification working.

But it can backfire two ways. First, negativity priming: before any notification, a customer assumes their order is fine, since people don't actively track shipments that seem normal. A message converts silent, unnoticed slack into an explicit negative event the customer now has to process. The PRD's own 1-7 day bucket already averages 2.71, well above the 1.70 floor, so a notification that treats a mild 2-day slip with disaster-level urgency can manufacture dissatisfaction in a segment that might have shrugged off a delay showing up quietly.

Second, and more serious: double deviation. A single failure is the delay. A double deviation is the delay plus a broken second promise, and it doesn't add to dissatisfaction, it compounds it, because the customer stops judging the delivery and starts judging the company. This risk is built directly into the current PRD scope. Section 4 has the notification carry a revised estimate. Section 7 admits the real carrier signal quality is unknown. Section 6 confirms v1's trigger is a weak heuristic (48 hours with no scan movement), not a calibrated ETA model. That means the "revised estimate" in v1 is low-confidence dressed up as a specific date. If that second date also slips, the customer has now been broken two promises on the same order, and that's a worse outcome than the 2.71-average "1-7 days late" experience the PRD is aiming for.

The false-positive guardrail in Section 5 also collapses two harms that aren't symmetric. A surprise-late order (today's baseline, no notification, arrives 7+ days late) stacks a bad outcome with total silence, that's the 1.70 / 79.2% cell, the worst one specifically because both failure types compound. An over-warned order (told late, arrives on time) looks free on paper, nothing bad happened to the order, but it has a real cost the guardrail doesn't price: it taxes the credibility of the next notification, the same "cry wolf" pattern seen in weather and security alerts, where a repeat false alarm costs far more than a first one. Given that every late order today is a guaranteed false negative under the current no-notification baseline, v1 should probably tolerate a fairly generous false-positive rate in exchange for catching more of the 7+ day bucket, then tighten as signal quality improves. The guardrail as written just says "stay under a threshold" without saying which direction to err.

The single biggest gap in v1 scope: the PRD scopes when to notify (trigger logic) and where (email), but never what the message says and with what confidence. A confidence-appropriate estimate (a range, not a precise-but-likely-wrong date), message tone, and a plan for when the revised estimate itself slips are all missing. So is a success metric that can catch the double-deviation failure mode specifically, Section 5 measures notified-versus-not in aggregate, which can show a positive average lift while masking a sub-population where a wrong revised estimate made things worse than silence would have.

## Measurement quality: the review score usually predates the delivery it's rating

This is the sharpest data-quality finding and it changes how the whole premise should be read. Checking `review_answer_timestamp` against `order_delivered_customer_date` for each bucket in `sql/04`, independently re-verified against the live database rather than trusted from a single pass:

| Delivery bucket | Orders | Review answered before actual delivery |
|---|---|---|
| 7+ days late | 2,797 | 97.3% |
| 1-7 days late | 3,612 | 49.2% |
| On the estimated day | 2,748 | 1.0% |
| Early (1-7 days) | 20,680 | 0.4% |
| Early (8+ days) | 66,516 | 0.3% |

For the bucket the whole PRD is built on, 97.3% of customers submitted their score before the package arrived. This isn't an edge case, it's nearly the entire bucket. This matches how Olist's review survey actually works: it's triggered off the estimated delivery date, not off actual receipt, so once an order is running badly behind schedule the survey typically goes out and gets answered while the customer is still waiting.

This means Sections 1 and 2 of the PRD have the sequence backwards for most of the late bucket. They frame 1.70 as customers reacting to having received a late delivery. The data shows the opposite for 97% of these orders: the customer is rating "my order still hasn't shown up and is overdue," not "I got it late and didn't like it." That doesn't kill the case for a notification, if anything it strengthens a different version of it, since the pain here is silence during an open-ended wait, exactly what a proactive "delayed, new ETA is X" message addresses, independent of when the package eventually lands. But it breaks Section 5's success metric as scoped. Comparing review score for notified-versus-not-notified against final delivery date could show no movement even if the notification genuinely helps, simply because most customers rate before the outcome is known. The metric needs to compare against when the review was actually submitted relative to the notification, not against delivery date.

Two smaller notes from the same pass, independently verified against the live database: 646 of 96,470 delivered orders (0.67%) have no matching review row and get silently dropped by `sql/04`'s inner join, and 547 orders have duplicate review rows, so the inner join slightly over-counts orders in whichever bucket they land in. Neither changes the headline numbers meaningfully, both should be stated rather than left implicit.

## What this changes

The core recommendation survives, with two real changes to how it's scoped and measured, not to whether it ships.

What doesn't survive as written: the language. "Confirmed the premise cleanly" in Section 8 overstates what a bivariate crosstab can show, and it should read as a strong, defensible association, not proven causation. The mean-score framing in the Goal section (1.70 toward 2.71 as a point gain) should move to a secondary role behind the 1-2 star share, which is the number that actually supports a cost model.

What does change scope: v1 needs a message-content and confidence-design workstream, not just trigger timing and a channel, because the double-deviation risk is built into the current plan (Section 4's "revised estimate" resting on a weak Section 6 trigger). And Section 5's success metric needs to key off review-submission timing, not delivery date, or it will measure nothing real given that 96.6% of the target bucket rates before the package arrives.

What doesn't change: the underlying case for telling customers about a delay instead of staying silent. Every lens here, even the ones arguing the causal story is confounded, converges on the same practical read, the pain in the worst bucket is largely about an unexplained, ongoing wait, and a notification addresses that regardless of whether the eventual mechanism is "customers punish lateness" or "bad sellers produce both slow shipping and bad reviews." The repeat-purchase and business-strategy critique doesn't argue against building this, it argues against calling it a retention play, and against letting it crowd out a cheap, parallel ops diagnostic in RJ and SP that has a higher ceiling if a fixable carrier or route issue exists there. None of the four lenses found a reason not to ship a rules-based v1 notification. They found reasons to describe it more honestly, measure it correctly, and design the message with real care.
